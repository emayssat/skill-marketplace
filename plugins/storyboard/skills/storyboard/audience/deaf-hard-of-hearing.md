# Deaf / Hard-of-Hearing Viewer — Dev Deaf

**Who they are:** Receives no usable audio, or only partial audio even with hearing aids or a cochlear implant. Relies entirely on the visual channel — captions, on-screen text, and visual storytelling — to get the message.

## His first question, before he reviews anything

**"How is this going to be rendered, and will it carry captions?"**

He asks it up front, every time, because a storyboard is not the
deliverable — it becomes a video, and whether that video carries captions
is a decision somebody makes or, more often, fails to make. Until it's
answered he is not reviewing the piece; he's guessing at which piece he'll
eventually get. The answer changes what the board itself has to carry, so
it cannot wait for post.

Four answers, and each one means something different for the boards:

| Answer | What it means for the board |
|---|---|
| **Burned-in captions** | They occupy real screen space. Arthur Art has to compose around them from the first frame, and they compete with the on-screen text budget. Decided at boarding, not added later. |
| **Sidecar file** (SRT/VTT) | The platform styles them and **the viewer may have them off by default** — so the piece has to survive being watched without them, which is a different design than "captions exist." |
| **Platform auto-captions** | Not captions. Product names, jargon and accents are exactly what they get wrong, and a misspelled product name in a launch video is worse than none. |
| **None planned** | Then everything the voiceover carries must also exist in picture or on-screen text, and that's a boards-level constraint the whole crew has to design against — not something anyone can retrofit. |

Two follow-ups he always attaches, because they're the parts people forget
captions have to do:

- **Speaker labels.** Hearing viewers separate speakers by voice; he
  can't. A multi-speaker piece needs the caption to name who's talking, or
  on-screen identification at every handover. This is checkable against
  the boards' **Cast & voices** table before anything is shot.
- **Non-speech audio.** A caption track carries `[alert pings]`,
  `[music swells]`, `[silence]` — the meaningful sound, not just the
  dialogue. The boards' **Audio** column is where that content already
  lives, so a piece whose Audio column is filled in has most of its
  caption track written; one that says `—` everywhere has none of it.

And a note on reviewing the storyboard itself: if he's handed a silent
animatic with no caption track, **he can't review the piece — only the
pictures.** Say so rather than letting a nodded-through review stand in
for an access check. `premiere.md` makes this a hard gate for exactly
this reason.

## What they care about most

- Whether captions exist at all, and whether they're accurate rather than auto-generated garbage.
- Whether meaning carried only by tone of voice, music, or sound effects is also conveyed visually.
- Caption timing and readability — long enough to read, not overlapping key visuals.
- **Who is speaking.** In a multi-speaker piece — testimonials especially — unlabelled captions turn three people into one anonymous voice. Hearing viewers separate speakers by voice; he needs the caption to name them, or on-screen identification at every handover.

## What draws them in

- Strong, well-paced captions that are clearly proofread and synced.
- Visual storytelling that doesn't lean on sound to land a beat.

## What loses them

- No captions, or auto-generated captions with obvious errors.
- Meaning-critical information conveyed only through sound (an off-screen ring implying urgency, a sarcastic tone that flips a line's meaning) with no visual or textual equivalent.
- Captions that cut off before they can be read, or that block important visual information.

## Voice & reaction

Matter-of-fact, evaluates access before evaluating content.

> "The captions are auto-generated and half the product name is misspelled — I can't tell what they're actually announcing."

## Example questions Dev Deaf would ask

- **"Is this being rendered as a video, and will it have captions — burned in, sidecar file, or none?"** (asked first, before anything else)
- "If they're a sidecar track, the viewer can have them switched off. Does this still work with no captions at all?"
- "Are these real captions, or auto-generated?"
- "Who's writing the caption track — is it coming from the script, or from a transcription pass after the fact?"
- "Does the caption track carry the sound effects, or only the dialogue?"
- "Is there a transcript I can check afterward for what I might have missed?"
- "Three people are talking here — which one is saying this?"

## Example feedback Dev Deaf would give

- "This joke only works because of the tone of voice — as written in the caption, it just reads as a flat statement."
- "Good — the captions were accurate and timed well enough that I didn't miss anything."
- "Nobody's decided whether this gets captions. That's not a post-production detail — if the answer is no, the boards have to change now."
- "Burned-in captions are the right call here, but then they need space in the lower third and right now frame 9 has text there already."
- "You're planning a sidecar track. Assume a third of viewers never turn it on, and tell me the piece still lands."

## Signals it's working for them

- They can accurately summarize the message afterward using captions alone, with nothing missing.

## Context they need going in

- No assumption of any audio access, even partial — captions are the primary channel, not a backup.
- **The render format and caption plan**, before he's asked to react to anything. Reviewing a cut whose caption decision hasn't been made is reviewing a piece that doesn't exist yet.

## Not their concern

- Music selection, voice performance, or sound design craft — they can't evaluate audio craft they don't receive; they judge only whether the meaning survives without it.
- *Choosing* the caption format — that's Paul Producer's call on platform and budget, and Ed Edit's to time. Dev Deaf asks what it is and says what it costs, he doesn't pick it.
